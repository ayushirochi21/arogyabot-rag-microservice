9. LEPROSY
1. Disease Name
Leprosy
2. Programme
National Leprosy Eradication Programme (NLEP), Ministry of Health & Family
Welfare, Government of India
3. Overview
• Leprosy is a chronic infectious disease.
• The disease primarily affects the skin and peripheral nerves.
• Leprosy can cause nerve damage, loss of sensation, muscle weakness and
disability if diagnosis and treatment are delayed.
• Leprosy is curable with multidrug therapy (MDT).
• The National Leprosy Eradication Programme provides diagnosis and treatment
services free of cost through public health facilities.
• The current revised classification and treatment protocol was implemented
throughout India from 1 April 2025.
4. Causative Organism
• Mycobacterium leprae is the causative organism of leprosy.
• The organism primarily affects peripheral nerves and the skin.
5. Transmission
• Transmission is associated mainly with prolonged exposure to an untreated
person with leprosy.
• The respiratory tract is an important route involved in transmission.
• Respiratory droplets from an untreated infectious person can transmit the
organism.
• The risk of transmission decreases substantially after effective treatment is
started.
• The incubation period of leprosy is long and variable.
6. Main Signs and Symptoms
Skin-related signs
• Pale or hypopigmented skin patches.
• Reddish skin patches.
• Loss or reduction of sensation over a skin lesion.
• Thickened or infiltrated skin.
• Nodules may occur in some forms of disease.
Nerve-related signs
• Thickened peripheral nerves.
• Numbness.
• Loss of sensation.
• Muscle weakness.
• Nerve pain or tenderness.
• Weakness of muscles supplied by affected nerves.
Other possible manifestations
• Eye involvement.
• Nasal involvement.
• Ulcers caused by loss of sensation.
• Deformities caused by prolonged nerve damage.
7. Cardinal Signs Used for Diagnosis
A diagnosis of leprosy is supported by the presence of characteristic clinical or
laboratory findings.
Cardinal Sign 1
• A pale or reddish skin lesion with definite loss of sensation.
Cardinal Sign 2
• Peripheral nerve involvement.
• Nerve involvement may include nerve thickening.
• Nerve involvement may be associated with loss of sensation.
• Nerve involvement may be associated with weakness of muscles supplied by the
affected nerve.
Cardinal Sign 3
• Demonstration of Mycobacterium leprae in an appropriate laboratory
specimen.
8. Revised Classification Under NLEP
Feature Paucibacillary (PB) Multibacillary (MB)
Skin lesions 1–5 More than 5
Nerve involvement No nerve involvement One or more nerves involved
Bacilli in slit-skin smear Absent/negative Present/positive
The revised classification emphasizes lesion count, nerve involvement and laboratory
findings.
9. Diagnosis
Diagnosis is based primarily on clinical assessment.
Important diagnostic components include:
• Examination of skin lesions.
• Testing sensation over suspected skin lesions.
• Examination of peripheral nerves.
• Assessment of sensory function.
• Assessment of motor function.
• Slit-skin smear or other appropriate laboratory testing when indicated.
10. Treatment
• Leprosy is treated with multidrug therapy (MDT).
• The current NLEP protocol uses a three-drug MDT regimen.
• The three drugs are:
o Rifampicin
o Dapsone
o Clofazimine
• The revised protocol uses the three-drug regimen for both PB and MB cases.
• Current treatment duration under the revised NLEP protocol is:
o PB: 6 months
o MB: 12 months
• Treatment should be completed according to the prescribed regimen.
• Diagnosis and treatment services are provided free of cost through public health
facilities.
11. Leprosy Reactions
Leprosy reactions are inflammatory episodes that may occur before, during or after
treatment.
Type 1 Reaction
• Also called a reversal reaction.
• Existing skin lesions may become red, swollen, warm or tender.
• Nerve inflammation may occur.
• Nerve pain or tenderness may occur.
• Loss of nerve function can worsen during a reaction.
Type 2 Reaction
• Also called erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL).
• More commonly associated with multibacillary disease.
• May produce tender skin nodules.
• Fever may occur.
• Joint symptoms may occur.
• Nerve involvement may worsen.
12. Complications
Untreated or inadequately managed leprosy may result in:
• Permanent nerve damage.
• Loss of sensation.
• Muscle weakness.
• Paralysis.
• Hand deformity.
• Foot deformity.
• Ulcers.
• Eye complications.
• Permanent disability.
13. Disability Prevention
NLEP emphasizes:
• Early detection.
• Early initiation of treatment.
• Monitoring of nerve function.
• Prevention of disability.
• Medical rehabilitation.
• Management of complications.
• Reconstructive surgery for selected cases requiring specialized care.
14. Prevention and Control
Important programme strategies include:
• Early detection of new cases.
• Complete treatment.
• Household contact surveys.
• Contact screening.
• Post-exposure prophylaxis for eligible contacts.
• Health education.
• Community awareness.
• Reduction of stigma and discrimination.
• Surveillance and reporting.
• Disability prevention and rehabilitation.
15. When to Seek Medical Evaluation
Medical evaluation is recommended when a person develops:
• A persistent pale or reddish skin patch with reduced sensation.
• Numbness.
• Loss of sensation in the hands or feet.
• Unexplained weakness of the hands or feet.
• Thickened or painful peripheral nerves.
• Recurrent painless injuries or ulcers associated with loss of sensation.
• New deformity of the hands, feet or eyes.
16. Key Public-Health Message
• Leprosy is curable.
• Early diagnosis helps prevent nerve damage and disability.
• Treatment is available free of cost through public health facilities under NLEP.
• Persons affected by leprosy should not be stigmatized or discriminated against.

10. CHOLERA
1. Disease Name
Cholera
2. Source
National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC), Ministry of Health & Family Welfare,
Government of India
3. Overview
• Cholera is an acute enteric infection.
• It is caused by Vibrio cholerae.
• Infection occurs through ingestion of contaminated food or water.
• Cholera is strongly associated with inadequate sanitation and unsafe water.
• Severe cholera can cause sudden, profuse watery diarrhoea.
• Severe fluid loss can lead to dehydration and death.
• Outbreaks can spread rapidly because of the short incubation period.
• With appropriate and timely treatment, cholera can be successfully managed.
4. Causative Organism
• Vibrio cholerae is the causative bacterium.
• Infection occurs when the organism is ingested through contaminated food or
water.
5. Transmission
• Cholera is transmitted mainly through contaminated water.
• Cholera can also be transmitted through contaminated food.
• Faecal contamination of drinking-water sources is an important route of
transmission.
• Poor sanitation increases transmission.
• Unsafe food preparation and handling can contribute to spread.
• Flooding can increase the risk of contamination of drinking-water sources.
• Population displacement and overcrowding can increase outbreak risk.
6. Incubation Period
• The incubation period can be very short.
• NCDC material describes an incubation period ranging from approximately 2
hours to 5 days.
• The short incubation period can contribute to rapid outbreak expansion.
7. Symptoms
Main symptom
• Acute watery diarrhoea.
Characteristic severe presentation
• Profuse watery diarrhoea.
• Rice-water stools may occur.
• Vomiting may occur.
• Rapid loss of body fluids may occur.
Severe disease
• Severe dehydration.
• Weakness.
• Circulatory compromise.
• Death can occur rapidly if severe dehydration is not treated.
NCDC identifies profuse diarrhoea with rice-water stools as a clinical pattern that
should raise suspicion of cholera.
8. Asymptomatic Infection
• A substantial proportion of people infected with cholera may not develop
symptoms.
• Asymptomatic infected individuals can still contribute to environmental
contamination through shedding of the organism in faeces.
9. Clinical Suspicion
Cholera should be considered when a person has:
• Profuse acute watery diarrhoea.
• Rice-water stools.
• Vomiting associated with severe diarrhoea.
• Rapid development of dehydration.
• A compatible illness during a known or suspected outbreak.
• A history of exposure to potentially contaminated water or food.
10. Major Complication
The major immediate complication of severe cholera is:
Rapid dehydration due to large-volume fluid loss.
Severe dehydration can progress to:
• Circulatory failure.
• Shock.
• Organ dysfunction.
• Death.
11. Treatment
Rehydration
• Rehydration is essential.
• Oral rehydration is used when the patient can drink adequately.
• Intravenous fluid replacement is required when dehydration is severe or oral
replacement is inadequate.
Antibiotic therapy
• Antibiotics are used as adjunctive therapy in appropriate cases.
• NCDC's National Treatment Guidelines list doxycycline as a first-line option for
adults in the cited treatment guidance.
• Azithromycin is listed for children and pregnant women in the cited guideline.
• Alternative antibiotic options are also provided in the NCDC guideline.
• Antibiotic selection should follow current clinical guidance and patient-specific
considerations.
Treatment principle
Replace fluid and electrolytes first and rapidly.
12. Outbreak Management
NCDC identifies the following important outbreak-control measures:
• Epidemiological investigation.
• Establishment of cholera treatment centres/units where required.
• Appropriate isolation facilities for seriously ill patients.
• Provision of safe drinking water.
• Provision of safe food.
• Improvement of sanitation.
• Proper disposal of human waste and sewage.
• Appropriate disinfection.
• Health education.
• Surveillance and monitoring.
13. Prevention
Safe Water
• Drink water from a safe source.
• Disinfect drinking water when contamination is suspected.
• During flood situations, appropriate water disinfection and chlorination are
important.
Food Safety
• Cook food thoroughly.
• Reheat food thoroughly when required.
• Eat freshly prepared hot food where possible.
• Boil milk before drinking.
• Avoid uncooked food from unsafe sources.
• Avoid ice from unreliable sources.
Hand Hygiene
• Wash hands after contact with faecal matter.
• Wash hands before preparing food.
• Wash hands before eating.
Sanitation
• Dispose of human excreta safely.
• Prevent faecal contamination of drinking-water sources.
• Maintain proper sanitation facilities.
14. Vaccination
• Cholera vaccines may be used as an additional public-health measure in
appropriate settings.
• Vaccination should not replace safe water, sanitation, hygiene and appropriate
outbreak-control measures.
• Mass chemoprophylaxis is not recommended as a general strategy for
controlling cholera outbreaks according to the NCDC cholera guidance.
15. High-Risk Situations
Risk increases with:
• Unsafe drinking water.
• Poor sanitation.
• Flooding.
• Population displacement.
• Crowding.
• Unsafe food handling.
• Disruption of water and sanitation infrastructure.
16. When to Seek Immediate Medical Care
Immediate medical assessment is required for:
• Profuse watery diarrhoea.
• Repeated vomiting.
• Signs of dehydration.
• Severe weakness.
• Dizziness or collapse.
• Reduced urine output.
• Rapid worsening of diarrhoea.
17. Key Public-Health Message
• Cholera is preventable and treatable.
• Rapid rehydration is the most important component of management.
• Safe drinking water, sanitation and hygiene are central to prevention.
• Suspected severe cholera should be treated urgently.

11. JAPANESE ENCEPHALITIS
1. Disease Name
Japanese Encephalitis (JE)
2. Source
National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC), Ministry of Health & Family Welfare,
Government of India
3. Overview
• Japanese encephalitis is a mosquito-borne viral disease.
• It is a zoonotic disease.
• Japanese encephalitis can cause inflammation of the brain.
• Severe disease can cause neurological complications and death.
• Japanese encephalitis is one of the causes of Acute Encephalitis Syndrome
(AES).
• NCDC maintains technical material covering diagnosis, treatment, surveillance
and prevention of JE/AES.
4. Causative Agent
• Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) is the causative virus.
• JEV is an arbovirus.
• The virus is maintained in an animal–mosquito transmission cycle.
5. Vector and Transmission
• Japanese encephalitis is transmitted by infected mosquitoes.
• Culex mosquitoes are important vectors.
• The transmission cycle involves mosquitoes and animal hosts.
• Pigs and certain water birds are important amplifying/reservoir hosts in the
natural cycle.
• Humans can become infected after being bitten by an infected mosquito.
• Human-to-human transmission through ordinary contact does not occur.
Transmission Sequence
Animal reservoir/amplifying host → infected mosquito → mosquito bite → human
infection
6. Epidemiological Pattern
• JE transmission is associated with mosquito abundance.
• Transmission may increase during and after the monsoon period in affected
areas.
• Environmental factors such as waterlogging and paddy cultivation can influence
mosquito breeding.
• Presence of pigs and migratory/water birds can be relevant to transmission
ecology.
7. Clinical Presentation
Japanese encephalitis may present with:
• Fever.
• Headache.
• Altered mental status.
• Neurological symptoms.
• Encephalitis.
• Seizures.
• Coma in severe disease.
Not every person infected with JEV develops encephalitis.
8. Acute Encephalitis Syndrome
AES is a clinical syndrome rather than a single disease.
• Japanese encephalitis is one of the important causes of AES.
• Other infectious and non-infectious causes can also produce AES.
• A patient with suspected encephalitis requires urgent clinical evaluation.
• Laboratory investigation is important to identify the cause.
9. Laboratory Diagnosis
Serological testing
• Detection of JEV-specific IgM antibodies by IgM-capture ELISA is an important
diagnostic method.
• JEV-specific IgM can be detected in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or serum.
• Detection of JEV-specific IgM in CSF provides strong evidence of JE infection.
• Serum results require careful interpretation in areas with high JE vaccination
coverage because antibodies may reflect previous infection or vaccination.
Molecular testing
• Viral RNA or genomic sequences may be detected in appropriate clinical
specimens.
• Molecular testing can also be used for characterization of circulating virus
genotypes.
Virus isolation
• Virus isolation can be performed using appropriate clinical or mosquito
specimens in specialized laboratories.
• Laboratory confirmation should be performed using appropriate validated
methods.
10. Treatment
• There is no specific antiviral treatment for Japanese encephalitis.
• Treatment is primarily supportive.
• Early referral to a hospital is important.
• Treatment should not be delayed while waiting for serological laboratory results
in a clinically suspected encephalitis case.
Supportive Management
Important management principles include:
• Maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance.
• Maintaining a clear airway.
• Providing oxygen when clinically required.
• Managing convulsions appropriately.
• Providing adequate nursing care.
• Monitoring neurological status.
• Providing hospital-level care for severe cases.
11. Severe Disease
Severe JE may lead to:
• Severe encephalitis.
• Seizures.
• Altered consciousness.
• Coma.
• Neurological impairment.
• Death.
12. Prevention
Vector Control
NCDC identifies vector control as an important component of JE prevention.
Measures include:
• Reduction of mosquito breeding.
• Vector surveillance.
• Appropriate insecticide-based vector-control measures.
• Environmental management.
• Reduction of mosquito–human contact.
Vaccination
• Vaccination is an important component of the national JE prevention and control
programme.
• Vaccination strategies are implemented according to national programme
recommendations and affected-area risk.
13. Surveillance
JE/AES surveillance includes:
• Detection of suspected AES cases.
• Laboratory investigation.
• Confirmation of JE cases.
• Monitoring of cases and deaths.
• Monitoring of vaccination status.
• Vector surveillance.
• Assessment of environmental risk factors.
• Monitoring of geographical and seasonal patterns.
14. Risk Factors and Environmental Factors
Factors associated with JE transmission include:
• Increased mosquito density.
• Waterlogged areas.
• Paddy fields.
• Presence of pig farms.
• Presence of migratory birds.
• Seasonal increases in mosquito populations.
• Increased mosquito–human contact.
15. When to Seek Urgent Medical Care
Urgent medical care is required when fever is associated with:
• Altered consciousness.
• Confusion.
• Severe headache.
• Seizures.
• Abnormal behaviour.
• Neurological weakness.
• Neck stiffness.
• Coma.
• Rapid neurological deterioration.
16. Key Public-Health Message
• Japanese encephalitis is a serious mosquito-borne disease.
• Severe disease can cause encephalitis and neurological complications.
• Suspected encephalitis requires early hospital referral.
• There is no specific antiviral treatment for JE.
• Prevention depends on vaccination, surveillance and mosquito-control
measures.

12. KALA-AZAR / VISCERAL LEISHMANIASIS
1. Disease Name
Kala-azar
2. Medical Name
Visceral Leishmaniasis (VL)
3. Source
Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India — National Center for
Vector Borne Diseases Control (NCVBDC), Kala-azar Elimination Programme
4. Overview
• Kala-azar is also called visceral leishmaniasis.
• It is an infectious disease caused by Leishmania parasites.
• The disease is transmitted through the bite of an infected sandfly.
• Kala-azar primarily affects the internal organs and reticuloendothelial system.
• The disease commonly presents with prolonged fever, splenomegaly and weight
loss.
• Untreated kala-azar can be fatal.
• Kala-azar is a major target of the national elimination programme.
5. Causative Agent
• Kala-azar is caused by Leishmania parasites.
• The parasite responsible for visceral leishmaniasis belongs to the Leishmania
donovani complex in the Indian subcontinent.
6. Vector
• Kala-azar is transmitted by infected female sandflies.
• Sandflies acquire the parasite while feeding on an infected host.
• An infected sandfly can transmit the parasite during a subsequent blood meal.
Transmission Sequence
Infected host → sandfly acquires parasite → parasite develops in sandfly → infected
sandfly bites human → human infection
7. Main Symptoms
The main clinical features include:
• Prolonged fever.
• Irregular fever.
• Weight loss.
• Enlarged spleen.
• Enlarged liver.
• Weakness.
• Anaemia.
• Progressive deterioration in untreated disease.
NCVBDC programme material identifies prolonged fever, splenomegaly and weight loss
as the major clinical features of kala-azar.
8. Suspected Case
A kala-azar suspect is generally identified when a person from an endemic area has:
• Fever for more than two weeks.
• Enlarged spleen.
• Relevant epidemiological exposure.
• Lack of response to an appropriate course of antimalarial treatment when
malaria has been considered.
The national programme uses a standardized clinical case definition before diagnostic
testing.
9. Diagnosis
Rapid Diagnostic Test
• A quality-assured rK39-based rapid diagnostic test (RDT) is used in the
programme for appropriate suspected cases.
• The test detects antibodies associated with visceral leishmaniasis.
• The rK39 test is used as an important field-level diagnostic tool.
Parasitological Confirmation
Parasitological confirmation can involve detection of the parasite in appropriate
specimens.
Possible specimens include:
• Bone marrow aspirate.
• Splenic aspirate.
• Other appropriate tissue/material according to clinical and laboratory facilities.
• Parasitological testing should be performed by appropriately trained personnel
at suitable facilities.
10. Important Diagnostic Consideration
• rK39-based testing should be used within the appropriate clinical case
definition.
• A positive antibody test should be interpreted in the appropriate clinical and
epidemiological context.
• Special situations such as relapse, reinfection and HIV–VL co-infection require
appropriate specialist evaluation.
• Patients with high clinical suspicion and a negative rapid test may require further
diagnostic evaluation.
11. Treatment
• Kala-azar requires specific anti-leishmanial treatment.
• The national programme has introduced treatment regimens based on effective
anti-leishmanial medicines.
• The NCVBDC operational guidelines identify liposomal amphotericin B as an
important treatment option.
• Other treatment options described in programme guidelines include:
o Miltefosine.
o Amphotericin B.
o Combination treatment with appropriate anti-leishmanial medicines.
• Treatment selection depends on the national programme protocol, clinical
condition and relevant patient factors.
12. Treatment Follow-up
Treatment outcomes should be monitored.
The kala-azar elimination programme distinguishes treatment outcomes based on:
• Initial outcome at completion of drug treatment.
• Final outcome during subsequent follow-up.
Patients should remain under appropriate follow-up after treatment to identify
treatment failure or relapse.
13. Post-Kala-Azar Dermal Leishmaniasis (PKDL)
• PKDL is a dermal manifestation that can occur after visceral leishmaniasis.
• It produces skin manifestations.
• PKDL may resemble other skin diseases.
• PKDL can sometimes resemble leprosy.
• Identification and treatment of PKDL are important for the elimination
programme because affected individuals can contribute to parasite
transmission.
• NCVBDC provides specific guidelines for PKDL diagnosis and treatment.
14. Complications
Untreated or inadequately treated kala-azar may lead to:
• Progressive weakness.
• Severe wasting and weight loss.
• Anaemia.
• Enlargement of the spleen and liver.
• Severe systemic illness.
• Death.
Kala-azar is considered potentially fatal when untreated.
15. Prevention and Control
Early Case Detection
• Active case search is used in programme areas.
• Passive case detection occurs through health facilities.
• Suspected cases are evaluated and tested promptly.
• Early diagnosis reduces the period during which untreated infection can
contribute to transmission.
Treatment
• Confirmed cases should receive appropriate complete treatment.
• Treatment outcomes should be monitored.
Vector Control
Integrated vector management is an important component of the elimination
programme.
Measures include:
• Indoor residual spraying where indicated.
• Appropriate insecticide use.
• Vector surveillance.
• Reduction of human–sandfly contact.
• Environmental measures where appropriate.
• Monitoring of vector density and transmission risk.
16. Surveillance
Kala-azar surveillance includes:
• Passive case detection.
• Active case search.
• Reporting of kala-azar cases.
• Reporting of PKDL cases.
• Vector surveillance.
• Monitoring of disease trends.
• Mapping of affected areas.
• Use of surveillance information for planning vector-control activities.
17. Risk Factors
Risk of kala-azar is associated with:
• Residence in endemic areas.
• Exposure to infected sandflies.
• Conditions that increase human–vector contact.
• Poverty and poor living conditions.
• Environmental conditions favourable to sandfly transmission.
18. When to Seek Medical Evaluation
Medical evaluation is recommended for a person from an endemic area who develops:
• Fever lasting more than two weeks.
• Enlarged spleen.
• Unexplained weight loss.
• Persistent weakness.
• Anaemia or pallor.
• Persistent fever that does not respond appropriately to treatment for other
suspected causes.
19. Key Public-Health Message
• Kala-azar is a serious but treatable disease.
• Early diagnosis and complete treatment are essential.
• Sandfly control is an important part of prevention.
• Active and passive surveillance support elimination.
• PKDL detection and management are important components of the elimination
programme.
