10.SEASONAL INFLUENZA
Overview
Seasonal influenza, commonly called flu, is a contagious respiratory illness caused by
influenza viruses. It commonly presents as an acute respiratory illness with sudden
cough, sore throat and fever, along with symptoms such as body aches, headache
and fatigue. Most uncomplicated cases recover with supportive care, but influenza can
become severe and lead to complications, particularly in people at higher risk.
Key Facts
• Seasonal influenza is a contagious respiratory illness caused by influenza
viruses.
• Typical influenza-like illness (ILI) includes sudden cough and sore throat with
measured fever ≥100.4°F, with onset within the previous 10 days.
• Other symptoms may include:
o Muscle/body aches
o Headache
o Fatigue
o Runny or stuffy nose
o Vomiting
o Diarrhea, particularly in children
• Fever and systemic symptoms usually last around 3 days, although they may
occasionally persist for 5–8 days.
• Cough and fatigue/malaise may persist for more than two weeks.
• Complete recovery may take 1–2 weeks or longer, particularly in older adults.
Symptoms
Common symptoms of influenza include:
• Fever
• Cough
• Sore throat
• Body or muscle aches
• Headache
• Fatigue
• Runny or blocked nose
• Vomiting
• Diarrhea, especially in children
Influenza symptoms usually begin suddenly rather than developing gradually.
Course of Illness
Early illness → Fever + respiratory/systemic symptoms → Recovery
• Fever and systemic symptoms generally last about 3 days.
• In some patients, symptoms may persist for 5–8 days.
• Cough and malaise can continue for more than two weeks.
• Older adults may require a longer period for complete recovery.
Patient Categorization
One of the most useful features of the NCDC guideline is its Category A, B and C
classification.
Category A — Mild / Uncomplicated
Patients may have:
• Mild fever
• Cough and/or sore throat
• With or without:
o Body aches
o Headache
o Diarrhea
o Vomiting
NCDC guidance:
• Influenza testing is not required for Category A patients.
• They generally do not require oseltamivir.
• Symptomatic treatment is provided.
• The patient should be monitored and reassessed by a doctor within 24–48 hours.
• Home isolation and avoidance of contact with the public and high-risk family
members are advised.
Category B — More Symptomatic / Higher Risk
Category B includes patients with more significant symptoms and/or those belonging to
higher-risk groups.
Important risk factors include:
• High-grade fever
• Severe sore throat
• Persistent cough
• Underlying medical conditions
• Pregnancy
• Young children
• Older adults
Patients in Category B require medical evaluation and appropriate treatment according
to clinical assessment.
Category C — Severe Disease
Category C represents patients with severe symptoms or signs of progressive
disease.
These patients require:
• Immediate medical evaluation
• Appropriate investigations
• Antiviral treatment as clinically indicated
• Immediate hospitalization when Category C criteria are met.
Important Warning Signs
Seek urgent medical attention when influenza symptoms are accompanied by:
• Difficulty in breathing or breathlessness
• Severe or progressive illness
• Signs of complications
• Worsening of an underlying chronic disease
• Other clinical features suggesting severe influenza
High-Risk Groups
NCDC identifies the following groups as being at increased risk of complications:
• Adults ≥65 years
• Pregnant women, including up to two weeks postpartum
• Children <5 years, particularly those <2 years
• People with chronic respiratory disease
• People with chronic heart, kidney, liver or neurological disease
• People with diabetes mellitus
• People with blood disorders, including haemoglobinopathies
• Immunosuppressed individuals, including people living with HIV/AIDS
• People receiving long-term corticosteroids
• Post-transplant patients
• People with extreme obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m²)
Complications
Severe or progressive influenza can produce a wide range of complications.
In children
Possible complications include:
• Otitis media
• Sinusitis
• Parotitis
• Pneumonia
• Croup
• Bronchiolitis
• Myocarditis
• Rhabdomyolysis
• Encephalopathy/encephalitis
• Invasive bacterial co-infection
• Reye syndrome associated with aspirin use
In adults
Possible complications include:
• Exacerbation of chronic diseases
• Pneumonia
• Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)
• Bronchitis
• Sinusitis
• Myocarditis
• Pericarditis
• Invasive bacterial co-infection
• Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis
Diagnosis and Testing
Diagnosis begins with clinical assessment and categorization.
NCDC's Seasonal Influenza resources include separate guidance for:
• Sample collection
• Laboratory procedures for influenza-virus detection
• Laboratory testing facilities
• Patient reporting
• Influenza surveillance.
Important: Not every patient with influenza-like illness requires laboratory testing.
NCDC's patient-categorization guideline specifically states that testing is not required
for Category A patients.
Laboratory testing is particularly relevant when clinically indicated and for surveillance,
severe cases and appropriate public-health investigations.
Treatment and Management
Management depends on the severity and category of illness.
For mild/uncomplicated illness
• Rest
• Adequate fluids
• Symptomatic treatment
• Home isolation
• Monitor symptoms
• Reassessment if symptoms worsen or fail to improve
Category A patients generally do not require oseltamivir according to the NCDC
categorization guidance.
For higher-risk or severe illness
Patients who fall into higher-risk or severe categories should be assessed by a
healthcare professional.
Oseltamivir/antiviral treatment should be used according to NCDC clinical
guidance and medical assessment, rather than through self-medication.
Category C patients should be immediately hospitalized.
Home Care and Isolation
NCDC's home-care guidance recommends that people with influenza-like illness or
appropriate influenza categories:
• Remain confined at home.
• Avoid mixing with the public.
• Avoid close contact with high-risk family members.
• Stay in a well-ventilated room, preferably separately from other family
members.
• Wear a mask as advised.
• Maintain appropriate respiratory hygiene.
• Follow medical advice regarding monitoring and treatment.
NCDC's home-care document advises home isolation for seven days and recommends
use of a disposable triple-layer mask where available.
Prevention
Prevention focuses on reducing transmission and protecting high-risk individuals.
Personal protection
• Wear a mask when appropriate, particularly around symptomatic individuals.
• Cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing.
• Wash hands regularly.
• Avoid close contact with people who are ill.
• Avoid unnecessary public exposure when experiencing influenza-like symptoms.
• Stay home while recovering according to medical/public-health advice.
Protect high-risk people
People with influenza symptoms should particularly avoid close contact with:
• Older adults
• Infants and young children
• Pregnant women
• People with chronic illnesses
• Immunocompromised individuals
Vaccination
NCDC maintains official guidance on seasonal influenza vaccine composition and
vaccination and periodically updates recommendations according to the circulating
influenza viruses and recommended vaccine composition. The NCDC website currently
lists the vaccine composition for the upcoming 2025–2026 season among its technical
resources.
When to Seek Medical Care
Medical evaluation should be sought when:
• Symptoms are severe or worsening.
• There is difficulty breathing or breathlessness.
• Fever or systemic symptoms persist or worsen.
• An underlying chronic disease becomes worse.
• The patient belongs to a high-risk group.
• There are signs of complications.
• The patient is a young child, older adult or pregnant.
• Symptoms do not improve as expected.
Severe/progressive cases should not be managed at home without medical
assessment.
