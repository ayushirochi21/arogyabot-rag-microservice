9. LEPROSY
Overview
Leprosy, also known as Hansen disease, is a chronic infectious disease caused mainly
by the bacterium Mycobacterium leprae. It primarily affects the skin and peripheral
nerves, but can also affect the mucosa of the upper respiratory tract and the eyes.
Leprosy is curable, and early diagnosis and treatment can prevent permanent disability.
Key Facts
• Causative organism: Mycobacterium leprae
• Type: Chronic infectious disease
• Mainly affects: Skin and peripheral nerves
• It can also affect the eyes and upper respiratory tract.
• Untreated disease can cause permanent nerve damage and disability.
• Leprosy is curable with multidrug therapy (MDT).
• The disease occurs in more than 120 countries.
• India, Brazil and Indonesia continue to report large numbers of new cases.
Transmission
Leprosy is believed to spread mainly through respiratory droplets from the nose and
mouth of an untreated person following prolonged and close contact.
Important:
Leprosy does NOT normally spread through casual contact, such as:
• Shaking hands
• Hugging
• Sharing meals
• Sitting next to someone
Once effective treatment is started, the patient stops transmitting the disease.
Transmission Flow
Untreated infected person
↓
Prolonged, close contact
↓
Respiratory droplets
↓
Transmission to another susceptible person
Symptoms and Signs
Symptoms can develop slowly. The disease commonly affects the skin and peripheral
nerves.
Common signs include:
• Pale or reddish skin patches
• Loss or reduction of sensation in affected skin
• Numbness
• Thickened or enlarged peripheral nerves
• Muscle weakness
• Loss of sensation in the areas supplied by affected nerves
• Eye involvement
• Nasal/upper respiratory involvement
If left untreated, nerve damage can result in deformity and permanent disability.
Diagnosis
WHO identifies three cardinal signs that can establish a diagnosis of leprosy:
1. A definite loss of sensation in a pale or reddish skin patch.
2. A thickened/enlarged peripheral nerve associated with loss of sensation and/or
muscle weakness.
3. Detection of leprosy bacilli in a slit-skin smear.
Classification
For treatment purposes, cases are classified as:
Paucibacillary (PB)
• 1–5 skin lesions
• No bacilli demonstrated in the skin smear.
Multibacillary (MB)
• More than 5 skin lesions, or
• Nerve involvement, or
• Bacilli demonstrated in a slit-skin smear.
Treatment
Leprosy is curable.
WHO recommends multidrug therapy (MDT) consisting of:
• Dapsone
• Rifampicin
• Clofazimine
Recommended duration:
• PB: 6 months
• MB: 12 months
Early diagnosis and prompt treatment help prevent disability.
Important
Treatment should be taken under the guidance of a healthcare professional.
Completing the prescribed treatment is essential.
Prevention and Control
Prevention focuses on early detection, treatment and contact screening.
WHO recommends:
• Early detection of cases
• Prompt initiation of MDT
• Screening of household, neighbourhood and social contacts
• Single-dose rifampicin as post-exposure prophylaxis (SDR-PEP) for eligible
contacts according to WHO recommendations.
Complications
Untreated leprosy can result in:
• Permanent nerve damage
• Muscle weakness
• Loss of sensation
• Deformities
• Disability
• Eye complications
• Social stigma and discrimination.
When to Seek Medical Care
Medical evaluation should be sought when a person develops:
• A persistent pale or reddish skin patch with reduced sensation
• Numbness
• Unexplained weakness of the hands or feet
• Thickened nerves
• Persistent skin changes associated with sensory loss
• Eye symptoms associated with suspected leprosy
Early diagnosis and treatment are important because nerve damage can become
permanent.

10. CHOLERA
Overview
Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal infection caused by consuming food or water
contaminated with the bacterium Vibrio cholerae.
It can cause severe acute watery diarrhoea and rapid dehydration. Without treatment,
severe cholera can become fatal within hours. However, most cases can be
successfully treated with oral rehydration solution (ORS).
Key Facts
• Causative organism: Vibrio cholerae
• Main route of transmission: Contaminated food or water
• Main symptom: Acute watery diarrhoea
• Severe disease can cause rapid dehydration.
• Most infections produce no or mild symptoms.
• Severe cases require rapid fluid replacement.
• Safe water, sanitation and hygiene are central to prevention.
• Oral cholera vaccines can help prevent and control outbreaks.
Transmission
Cholera is mainly transmitted through the faecal–oral route.
Typical transmission cycle:
Infected person → Faeces contaminate environment → Water/food contamination →
Another person consumes contaminated water/food → Infection
Poor sanitation and unsafe drinking water increase the risk of transmission.
Symptoms
Symptoms generally appear 12 hours to 5 days after infection.
Common symptoms:
• Acute watery diarrhoea
• Vomiting
• Rapid loss of body fluids
• Dehydration
• Weakness
Most infected people develop no symptoms or only mild/moderate diarrhoea.
A minority develop severe acute watery diarrhoea with life-threatening dehydration.
Dehydration — Major Danger
The most serious complication of cholera is severe dehydration caused by rapid fluid
loss.
Signs of significant dehydration can include:
• Extreme thirst
• Weakness
• Reduced urination
• Dry mouth
• Sunken eyes
• Dizziness
• Rapid deterioration
Severe dehydration is a medical emergency.
Diagnosis
Cholera should be suspected in a person with acute watery diarrhoea, particularly
during an outbreak or in an area where cholera transmission is occurring.
Laboratory testing can help confirm the presence of Vibrio cholerae.
WHO emphasizes the importance of epidemiological and laboratory surveillance for
detecting and monitoring outbreaks.
Treatment
The most important principle:
Replace lost fluids immediately.
Mild/Moderate illness
• Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is the main treatment.
• Continue appropriate feeding/nutrition.
• Monitor for worsening dehydration.
Severe illness
Severe cases require:
• Intravenous fluids
• ORS
• Antibiotics, when indicated
Rapid treatment is critical because severe cholera can progress very quickly.
Simple Treatment Flow
Watery diarrhoea
↓
Assess dehydration
↓
No/severe dehydration → ORS and monitoring
Severe dehydration → IV fluids + ORS + appropriate antibiotics
Prevention
Prevention focuses strongly on WASH — Water, Sanitation and Hygiene.
Safe water
• Drink safe water.
• Use appropriately treated water.
• Avoid potentially contaminated drinking water.
Food safety
• Eat properly prepared food.
• Keep food protected from contamination.
• Maintain hand hygiene before preparing or eating food.
Sanitation
• Use safe sanitation facilities.
• Dispose of human waste safely.
• Prevent contamination of drinking-water sources.
Vaccination
Oral cholera vaccines (OCV) can help prevent and control cholera, particularly as part
of outbreak-control and high-risk-area strategies.
Complications
The major complication is:
Severe dehydration → Shock → Death
Other consequences may include:
• Electrolyte disturbances
• Kidney injury associated with severe dehydration
• Circulatory collapse
Rapid rehydration can prevent most deaths.
When to Seek Urgent Care
Seek medical attention immediately for:
• Profuse watery diarrhoea
• Repeated vomiting
• Signs of dehydration
• Severe weakness
• Reduced urine
• Dizziness or fainting
• Rapid worsening of symptoms

11. JAPANESE ENCEPHALITIS
Overview
Japanese encephalitis (JE) is a mosquito-borne viral disease that causes inflammation
of the brain.
It is caused by Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), a flavivirus related to dengue, yellow
fever and West Nile viruses.
Most JEV infections are asymptomatic or cause only mild illness. However, a small
proportion progress to severe encephalitis, which can cause permanent neurological
disability or death.
Key Facts
• Causative agent: Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV)
• Virus family: Flavivirus
• Main vector: Culex mosquitoes
• Mainly affects: Central nervous system/brain
• Most infections are mild or asymptomatic.
• Severe disease can cause encephalitis, seizures, paralysis and coma.
• The majority of clinical cases occur in children below 15 years.
• There is no specific antiviral cure.
• Effective vaccines are available for prevention.
Transmission
JEV is mainly transmitted by infected Culex mosquitoes, particularly Culex
tritaeniorhynchus.
The virus circulates naturally between:
Mosquitoes ↔ pigs / water birds
Humans become infected when bitten by an infected mosquito.
Humans generally do not develop enough virus in their blood to infect feeding
mosquitoes, so humans are considered dead-end hosts.
Transmission Flow
JEV-infected mosquito
↓
Mosquito bites human
↓
JEV infection
↓
Usually asymptomatic/mild infection
OR
Severe encephalitis
Symptoms
The incubation period is approximately 4–14 days.
Most infections produce:
• No symptoms, or
• Mild fever
• Headache
In children, gastrointestinal pain and vomiting may be important early symptoms.
Severe Japanese Encephalitis
Severe disease may develop rapidly.
Warning symptoms include:
• High fever
• Severe headache
• Neck stiffness
• Disorientation
• Altered consciousness
• Seizures
• Spastic paralysis
• Coma
Severe encephalitis is a medical emergency.
Complications
Among people who develop encephalitis:
• Case-fatality can be as high as 30%.
• Around 30–50% of survivors may develop permanent neurological, cognitive or
behavioural problems.
Possible long-term problems include:
• Seizures
• Hearing loss
• Vision problems
• Speech/language difficulties
• Memory problems
• Communication difficulties
• Weakness or paralysis
• Cognitive and behavioural changes.
Diagnosis
A person living in or travelling to a JE-endemic area who develops encephalitis may be
suspected of having JE.
Diagnosis involves:
• Clinical assessment
• Evaluation of neurological symptoms
• Lumbar puncture, where appropriate
• Laboratory confirmation
WHO recommends detection of JEV-specific IgM antibodies using an IgM-capture
ELISA.
Testing of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is preferred because it can reduce false-positive
results associated with previous infection or vaccination.
Treatment
There is currently no specific antiviral treatment for Japanese encephalitis.
Treatment is supportive and focuses on:
• Stabilizing the patient
• Managing severe symptoms
• Supporting breathing and circulation when necessary
• Managing seizures and other complications
• Monitoring neurological status
• Providing appropriate intensive care when required.
Important
Suspected encephalitis requires urgent medical attention.
Prevention
1. Vaccination
Vaccination is the most important preventive measure in populations where JE is a
public-health concern.
WHO recommends JE vaccination as part of national immunization programmes in
areas where JE is recognized as a public-health priority.
2. Mosquito-bite prevention
• Use mosquito repellents.
• Wear clothing that reduces exposed skin.
• Use mosquito nets where appropriate.
• Reduce mosquito breeding sites.
• Take additional precautions in areas with active JE transmission.
High-Risk Settings
JE transmission is particularly associated with:
• Rural and periurban environments
• Areas with rice cultivation
• Areas where pigs and water birds are present
• Warm/tropical areas where mosquito populations increase
Transmission may intensify during rainy seasons in tropical and subtropical regions.
When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention
Immediate medical care is required when fever is accompanied by:
• Severe headache
• Neck stiffness
• Confusion/disorientation
• Altered consciousness
• Seizures
• Weakness/paralysis
• Coma
• Rapid neurological deterioration

12. KALA-AZAR (VISCERAL LEISHMANIASIS)
Overview
Kala-azar, also known as visceral leishmaniasis (VL), is the most serious form of
leishmaniasis.
It is caused by Leishmania parasites and transmitted through the bite of infected
female phlebotomine sandflies.
If untreated, visceral leishmaniasis can be fatal.
Key Facts
• Other name: Visceral leishmaniasis / Kala-azar
• Cause: Leishmania parasites
• Vector: Infected female phlebotomine sandflies
• Main organs affected: Spleen, liver and blood-forming system
• Major symptoms: Prolonged/irregular fever, weight loss, enlarged spleen and
liver, anaemia
• VL is the most severe form of leishmaniasis.
• Untreated VL is potentially fatal.
• It is treatable and curable when properly diagnosed and treated.
Transmission
The parasite is transmitted through the bite of an infected female sandfly.
Transmission Flow
Infected sandfly
↓
Bites human
↓
Leishmania parasites enter the body
↓
Parasites multiply in tissues
↓
Visceral leishmaniasis
The disease is associated with factors such as:
• Malnutrition
• Population displacement
• Poor housing
• Weak immune systems
• Poverty and limited access to healthcare.
Symptoms
The main clinical features of visceral leishmaniasis include:
• Irregular bouts of fever
• Significant weight loss
• Enlarged spleen (splenomegaly)
• Enlarged liver (hepatomegaly)
• Anaemia
• Weakness and fatigue
Symptoms can develop gradually and may resemble other diseases, so appropriate
medical evaluation is important.
Why Kala-Azar Is Serious
The disease affects internal organs and the body's immune and blood-forming systems.
Without treatment, visceral leishmaniasis can become life-threatening.
WHO states that VL is fatal in over 95% of cases when untreated.
Diagnosis
Diagnosis of visceral leishmaniasis involves combining:
Clinical assessment
Look for:
• Prolonged/irregular fever
• Weight loss
• Enlarged spleen
• Enlarged liver
• Anaemia
Laboratory confirmation
WHO recommends combining clinical findings with:
• Parasitological tests
• Serological tests, including rapid diagnostic tests where appropriate.
Simple Diagnostic Flow
Prolonged fever + weight loss + enlarged spleen/liver
↓
Clinical suspicion of VL
↓
Parasitological / serological testing
↓
Diagnosis confirmed
↓
Prompt complete treatment
Treatment
Visceral leishmaniasis is treatable and curable, but patients require appropriate
medical treatment.
Treatment depends on factors including:
• Geographic location
• Leishmania species
• Clinical condition
• Coexisting diseases
• Immune status.
Important
All patients diagnosed with visceral leishmaniasis require prompt and complete
treatment.
Treatment should be selected and administered by qualified healthcare professionals.
Prevention
Because transmission occurs through sandflies, prevention focuses on reducing
exposure to infected sandflies.
Personal protection
• Use insect repellents.
• Wear clothing that minimizes exposed skin.
• Use protective nets where appropriate.
• Reduce exposure to sandflies, particularly in endemic areas.
Environmental control
• Improve housing conditions.
• Reduce sandfly breeding/resting environments where feasible.
• Support community vector-control programmes.
Public-health measures
• Early detection
• Prompt treatment
• Surveillance
• Vector control
• Community awareness
Risk Factors
Risk is associated with:
• Poverty
• Malnutrition
• Poor housing
• Population displacement
• Weak immune system
• Living in or travelling to endemic areas.
Complications
Untreated or inadequately treated VL can lead to:
• Severe anaemia
• Progressive weakness and wasting
• Severe systemic illness
• Death
A further complication can occur after treatment in some endemic settings: post-kalaazar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL), which is characterized by skin manifestations
following VL. This requires appropriate clinical evaluation and management.
When to Seek Medical Care
Medical evaluation should be sought when a person in or from an endemic area
develops:
• Persistent or prolonged fever
• Unexplained weight loss
• Enlarged abdomen due to an enlarged spleen/liver
• Severe weakness
• Pallor or suspected anaemia
• Other symptoms suggestive of visceral leishmaniasis
Early diagnosis and complete treatment are essential.
