The exact source listed by ICMR is “Standard Treatment Workflow (STW) for the
Management of FEVER IN CHILDREN,” ICD-10-R50. ICMR currently lists it under

11. FEVER IN CHILDREN
Overview
Fever is a common clinical presentation in children and may occur due to a wide range
of infectious and non-infectious conditions. The ICMR Standard Treatment Workflow
emphasizes assessing the duration of fever, associated/localizing symptoms,
general appearance, vital signs and physical findings to identify the likely cause and
determine whether investigations, treatment, hospitalization or referral are required.
Definition of Fever
According to the ICMR STW:
• Core/rectal temperature ≥38.0°C (100.4°F) is considered fever.
• Axillary temperature >37.5°C is also considered fever.
What to Ask
When evaluating a child with fever, the ICMR workflow recommends asking about:
• Duration of fever
• Localizing symptoms suggesting:
o Respiratory tract infection
o Urinary tract infection
o Gastrointestinal infection
o Central nervous system infection
• Rash
• Joint symptoms
• Skin or soft-tissue swelling/redness
• Recent vaccination within 24 hours
• Drug or toxin exposure
• Similar illness among family members or in the neighbourhood.
Clinical Examination
Vital Signs
Assess:
• Temperature
• Heart rate
• Respiratory rate
• Blood pressure
• Capillary filling time (CFT)
General Appearance
Look for:
• Sick or toxic appearance
• Lethargy
• Irritability
• Inconsolability
• Dehydration
General Examination
Examine:
• Ear, nose and throat
• Skin and rash
• Lymph nodes
• Joints
• Genital area
• Bones
• Skin for pustules, pyoderma, impetigo or cellulitis.
Systemic Examination
The workflow includes examination of:
• Chest
• Abdomen
• Central nervous system
• Cardiovascular system.
Clinical Clues to Possible Diagnosis
The ICMR workflow provides specific symptom combinations that can help identify
possible causes.
Fever + Respiratory Symptoms
• Cough + runny nose → Upper respiratory tract infection (URTI)
• Membrane over tonsils/pharynx → Diphtheria
• Paroxysmal cough → Pertussis-like illness
• Barking cough → Laryngotracheobronchitis / croup
Fever + Rash
• Red maculopapular rash → Measles, rubella or dengue
• Fine generalized maculopapular rash + systemic dysfunction/shock →
Meningococcemia
• Itchy erythematous macules progressing to clear vesicles → Varicella
Fever + Other Findings
• Parotid swelling → Mumps
• Arthritis → Consider chikungunya, acute rheumatic fever or juvenile idiopathic
arthritis
• Strawberry-red tongue + skin peeling + lymphadenopathy + conjunctival
injection → Kawasaki disease.
Investigation of the Febrile Child
The ICMR STW emphasizes that investigations should be performed when the result is
expected to influence management.
Fever <7 Days
For a child with fever alone:
• If fever is <72 hours and the child does not appear sick:
o No investigations are generally required.
• If fever persists for >72 hours, investigations may include:
o Total leukocyte count (TLC)
o Differential leukocyte count (DLC)
o Peripheral smear for leukocyte morphology
o Malarial parasite testing
o Platelet count
Additional investigations may be considered according to the clinical situation.
Fever >7 Days
When fever persists for more than 7 days, particularly when localizing symptoms are
present, broader investigations may be considered based on clinical findings.
The ICMR workflow lists investigations such as:
• Serological testing for selected infections
• Bone marrow examination where indicated
• ANA profile
• HIV serology
• Echocardiography
• CT/PET imaging in selected situations
• Testing for infections such as Brucella, CMV, herpes and Japanese
encephalitis, depending on the clinical presentation.
Important: These are not tests that every child with fever should undergo. The ICMR
workflow uses them selectively according to fever duration, symptoms and clinical
suspicion.
Management
The ICMR workflow organizes management into five steps.
STEP 1 — Initial Assessment and Stabilization
• Identify and manage urgent problems.
• Reduce fever using paracetamol (15 mg/kg by any route) and/or hydrotherapy.
• Manage life-threatening conditions immediately.
• Consider the first dose of an antibiotic when meningitis, severe pneumonia or
severe malnutrition is suspected.
• Consider antimalarial treatment when malaria is suspected.
STEP 2 — Consider Hospitalization
Hospital observation/management should be considered for:
• All neonates
• Young infants with a toxic appearance
• Children with severe malnutrition
• Toxic appearance
• Inability to feed
• Lethargy
• Irritability
• Dehydration
• Fever/illness lasting >14 days without a diagnosis
• Any other situation where the treating physician considers hospitalization
necessary.
STEP 3 — Consider Referral to a Tertiary-Care Centre
After appropriate stabilization and/or initial management, referral should be considered
when there is:
• Need for intensive care
• Complex multisystem disease
• Confirmed complications of the primary illness.
STEP 4 — Empiric Management
Empiric treatment should be based on:
• Likely diagnosis
• Clinical severity/sickness status
• Availability of investigation facilities
Treatment should subsequently be modified according to investigation results and
local antimicrobial sensitivity patterns.
The ICMR STW specifically states that anti-tuberculosis treatment should not be
started empirically, except in suspected tuberculous meningitis.
STEP 5 — Consider Discharge When
Discharge may be considered when:
• The child has been afebrile for >48 hours or fever is showing defervescence.
• The child is feeding well.
• Symptoms other than fever have resolved or are resolving.
• The physician is satisfied that further care can safely continue on an ambulatory
basis.
• The required duration of IV antibiotic therapy has been completed.
When Urgent Medical Attention Is Needed
Based on the ICMR workflow, particular concern is warranted when a febrile child has:
• Toxic or very sick appearance
• Lethargy or marked irritability
• Inability to feed
• Dehydration
• Signs suggesting meningitis or severe pneumonia
• Severe malnutrition
• Signs of systemic dysfunction or shock
• Complex multisystem involvement
• Prolonged fever without an established diagnosis.
